\section{Other Direct computation agent spacing on the two-sphere} \label{annex:Other Direct computation agent spacing on the two-sphere}

Two other methods here discussed about point distribution of the direct computation method. Latitude/Longitude and Poisson Disk sampling, followed by three methods inspired in cyclical pursuit~\ref{subsec.2:Cyclic Pursuit} that do not work and finally the comparison between methods.

\subsection{Latitude and Longitude} \label{sec.2:Latitude and longitude}

The latitude--longitude lattice method considered here, heavily inspired by and influenced by \cite{Latitude_Longitude_vs_fibonacci}, consists of points located at the intersections of a grid of meridians and parallels, separated by equal angular increments in latitude. The longitude spacing, however, is allowed to vary between parallels to account for the convergence of the meridians toward the poles. This arrangement may be referred to as the \emph{latitude--longitude grid} or the \emph{equal-angle grid}. Due to the convergence of meridians toward the poles, the resulting distribution exhibits significant anisotropy.

The sphere is divided into \(N\) equal angular intervals in latitude, such that

$$
\delta = \frac{180^\circ}{N},
\qquad N \in \mathbb{N}.
$$
Consequently, there are \(N-1\) interior parallels in addition to the two poles. At a given colatitude \(\theta_i\), the number of longitude points is selected according to
$$
n_i = \operatorname{round}\left(2N\sin\theta_i\right),
\qquad
\theta_i = \frac{i\pi}{N},
\quad i=1,\ldots,N-1.
$$
Thus, the total number of points is
$$
\gls{n_D} =
2+
\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}
\operatorname{round}
\left(
2N\sin\frac{i\pi}{N}
\right).
$$

Unlike a conventional latitude--longitude grid with a fixed number of longitude points on every parallel, this approach reduces the number of points towards the poles, reflecting the decreasing circumference of the parallels. Nevertheless, the resulting distribution remains anisotropic because the points are generated according to the latitude--longitude parameterization.

For example, for \(N=29\), the procedure produces \(\gls{n_D}=1070\) points. As can be observed in Figure~\ref{fig.3:latlon_results_demonstration}, the resulting distribution is highly symmetric and structured. However, the convergence of the meridians produces non-uniform spacing between neighboring points, especially towards the poles.

\subsection{Poisson Disk sampling} \label{sec.3:Poisson sampling disk}

Poisson disk sampling is a stochastic sampling technique that enforces a minimum distance between samples, producing distributions that are both random and well-spaced. Formally, a Poisson disk distribution is defined such that no two samples are closer than a user-defined distance \gls{min_dist}. This property avoids clustering while preserving randomness, resulting in so-called \emph{blue noise} characteristics, which are highly desirable for a very isotropic distribution of agents.

Poisson disk samples are generated using the \textit{fast algorithm}~\cite{poisson_sampling_disk}. The method improves efficiency by restricting candidate samples to a neighborhood around existing points. A background grid is used to accelerate distance queries, with the cell size chosen as $\gls{min_dist} / \sqrt{n}$ in an $n$-dimensional domain, ensuring that each cell contains at most one sample.

The algorithm starts by placing an initial random sample in the domain and adding it to an active list. While this list is not empty, a sample is selected and up to \gls{P_k} candidate points are generated uniformly in the spherical annulus between radii \( r \) and \( 2r \). If a candidate is at least distance \gls{min_dist} from all existing samples, it is accepted and added to the active list. If no valid candidate is found after \gls{P_k} attempts, the sample is removed from the active list.

Because each iteration either adds or removes a sample and the number of candidate attempts is bounded by a constant, the overall computational complexity of the algorithm is \( O(N) \), being \( N \) the total number of samples. A key advantage of this approach is that it generalizes naturally to arbitrary dimensions, making it well suited for multi-dimensional sampling problems.

It may seem counterintuitive to classify a stochastic sampling technique alongside direct computation methods. However, despite its internal iterative procedure, Poisson disk sampling produces a final, fixed distribution once the algorithm completes. This contrasts with truly iterative methods, where point positions are continuously updated over time to gradually approach an evenly spaced configuration. Since Poisson disk sampling does not involve such progressive adjustment after generation, it is considered, in this work, a direct computation method.

For a desired fixed number of agents \gls{n_D}, a challenge arises because standard Poisson disk sampling does not guarantee an exact number of generated points. Owing to its low computational cost, such an approach could be practical for real-time or large-scale deployments. However, it should be noted that this remains a conceptual approach and has neither been proven feasible nor experimentally validated during the development of this thesis.

In Figure~\ref{fig.3:poisson_disk_sphere}, the results obtained with this method are presented. Poisson disk sampling produces highly isotropic, blue-noise distributions and generalizes naturally to arbitrary manifolds. In this case, however, the distribution exhibits some clustering near the poles of the sphere. This behavior is not an inherent limitation of the Poisson disk sampling algorithm, but rather a consequence of the surface representation and parameterization used in its implementation.

More specifically, the sphere is represented using a parameterization with singularities at the poles. Near these points, the correspondence between the parameter space and the actual spherical surface becomes degenerate: multiple directions in the parameterization converge toward the same location on the sphere. If neighborhood queries, distances, or point movements are computed directly in this parameter space, rather than using an appropriate intrinsic or geometrically consistent representation of the sphere, points near the poles may not be treated as close to one another in the same way as points elsewhere on the surface. This can lead to an accumulation of samples in these regions when the surface is wrapped around the sphere.

A representation that avoids such singularities, or an implementation that evaluates distances and neighborhoods directly on the manifold, would prevent this parameterization-induced artifact. Thus, the observed non-uniformity should be understood as an implementation-related limitation rather than a fundamental fault of the Poisson disk sampling method itself.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig.4:centalized_methods_points_on_sphere}, the three methods produce noticeably different agent distributions, a difference that the metrics in Tables~\ref{annex:Runtime comparison of point sampling methods} and~\ref{tab:sphere_spacing} make quantitative. The longitude \& latitude method can be discarded due to the limitation on the number of agent formations possible despite it producing the most regular spacing of the three methods ($d_{NN,\min}/d_{NN,\text{avg}} = 0.9712$, CV $= 0.0068$).

This leaves two viable options: the Fibonacci lattice and Poisson disk sampling. In terms of runtime, the Fibonacci lattice is by far the fastest of the two, completing in a mean of $1.075\times10^{-4}\,\text{s}$ against $7.51\times10^{-2}\,\text{s}$ for Poisson disk sampling (Table~\ref{annex:Runtime comparison of point sampling methods}) -- a factor of roughly $698\times$, consistent with Figure~\ref{fig.4:centalized_comparing_methods_box_shape}. The Fibonacci lattice is also markedly more consistent, with a standard deviation $21\times$ smaller than the lon--lat method's ($3.66\times10^{-5}\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $7.69\times10^{-4}\,\text{s}$) and over an order of magnitude smaller than Poisson disk sampling's ($1.4\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}$).

The spacing metrics in Table~\ref{tab:sphere_spacing} reinforce this choice. The Fibonacci lattice achieves a near-uniform nearest-neighbor spacing ($d_{NN,\min}/d_{NN,\text{avg}} = 0.9171$, CV $= 0.0105$), only marginally less regular than the lon--lat grid, and it attains the lowest Riesz energy of the three methods ($483586.9$ vs.\ $553166.4$ for lon--lat and $515388.4$ for Poisson disk), indicating the most electrostatically balanced point configuration. Poisson disk sampling, by contrast, shows a much lower minimum-to-average spacing ratio ($0.0640$) and a substantially higher coefficient of variation (CV $= 0.2533$), reflecting the greater irregularity inherent to its randomized placement, though it remains more versatile, since it can be applied to arbitrary geometries rather than being restricted to a spherical surface.

Taken together, the Fibonacci lattice offers the best combination of speed, runtime consistency, and spacing uniformity of the three methods, and was therefore chosen as the preferred distribution method for centralized systems.

\section{Alternative Agent Positioning Strategies} \label{annex:Alternative Agent Positioning Strategies}

This section discusses alternative methods that were considered to work to position agents on a spherical surface. Although the final configuration of each drone is based on the Fibonacci lattice, several other approaches were investigated, inspired by phase-separation methods commonly used for 2D formations \cite{Litimein2021,Jin2018,Kim2007}.  In particular, an attempt was made to develop a displacement-based 3D method in which each drone maintains a fixed angular separation from its predecessor. It was ultimately deemed unsuitable for the final deployment strategy, but is included here for completeness and comparison with the other attempted methods. Either way this approach was flawed since opposite of the 2D approach where all drones are keeping a distance from the previous and next, in 3D the "top" and "bottom" drones aren't associated in any way.

The standard Fibonacci lattice formulation could not be directly applied in this context. While the longitude increments between agents are constant, allowing a simple angular separation rule, the latitude is computed through a nonlinear $\arcsin(\cdot)$ mapping, which is inherently non-uniform. This nonlinearity prevents the use of a single, constant spacing rule across all agents. Several attempts were made to linearize this relationship so that all agents could rely on identical local control laws, but none yielded satisfactory results. For completeness, the tested approaches are summarized below.

\subsection{Mean Distance Between Points on the two-sphere}
In the first attempt, the reference spacing was taken as the mean of the Euclidean distances between consecutive-index points of the Fibonacci lattice (i.e., cyclical pursuit). This approach fails because consecutive indices are only spatially adjacent away from the poles: in the equal-area parameterization used to build the lattice, $\theta_i = \arcsin(2i/\gls{n_D})$, so
\[
\frac{d\theta}{di} = \frac{2/\gls{n_D}}{\sqrt{1-\left(2i/\gls{n_D}\right)^2}},
\]
which is small near the equator ($i \approx 0$) but diverges as $i \to \pm \gls{n_D}/2$, i.e., as $\theta_i \to \pm\pi/2$. Physically, this means a single step in index $i$ spans an increasingly large latitudinal arc near the poles in order to preserve equal area per point, so "consecutive" indices there are no longer near neighbors in space, they jump across a disproportionately large patch of the sphere, producing a handful of pairwise distances far larger than the typical near-neighbor spacing elsewhere on the lattice. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig.4:fibonacci_diff_methods} (row~2), this skews the mean: with 1001 points on a sphere of radius $\gls{r_desired} = 5$, the mean consecutive-index distance was approximately 7.3270, more than half the sphere's diameter, so it does not provide a meaningful or usable spacing reference.

\subsection{Mean Latitude Spacing}
Since the primary source of nonlinearity lies in the latitude distribution, a second approach considered using the average latitude increment instead of the $\arcsin(\cdot)$ mapping. However, this method introduces a different issue: the geodesic distance between agents increases as the effective radius of the latitude circle grows, particularly near $\theta = \pi$. This results in a pronounced clustering of points near longitudes $\phi = 0$ and $\phi = 2\pi$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig.4:fibonacci_diff_methods} (row~3). While the $\arcsin(\cdot)$ formulation avoids this clustering, the mean-latitude approach fails to preserve uniform surface spacing.

\subsection{Linearized Vertical Distribution with Uniform Angular Progression}
A final attempt aimed to mitigate the clustering near the poles by enforcing a uniform angular progression while simultaneously linearizing the vertical distribution. Instead of relying on the nonlinear Fibonacci latitude mapping, a constant latitude increment $\theta_{\text{mean}} = \pi / \gls{n_D}$ was imposed. In parallel, a constant longitude increment $\phi_{\text{mean}}$ was computed from the average difference of the Fibonacci-based longitudes.

The Cartesian coordinates of each agent were defined as
\begin{equation}
x_i = R \sin(i\theta_{\text{mean}})\cos(i\phi_{\text{mean}}), \quad
y_i = R \sin(i\theta_{\text{mean}})\sin(i\phi_{\text{mean}}),
\end{equation}
with the vertical coordinate distributed linearly according to
\begin{equation}
z_i = -R + \frac{R}{\gls{n_D}} + \frac{2R(i-1)}{\gls{n_D}},
\end{equation}
thereby enforcing equal spacing along the $z$-axis.

The motivation for this approach was twofold. First, by fixing $\theta_{\text{mean}}$, each agent would only need to maintain a constant angular separation from its predecessor. Second, by distributing agents uniformly along the vertical axis, the excessive clustering near the poles observed in previous methods could be reduced. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig.4:fibonacci_diff_methods} (row~4), this method successfully eliminates severe polar clustering and yields a visually more homogeneous vertical distribution.

However, despite these improvements, this approach still fails to guarantee uniform geodesic distances between neighboring agents. The imposed linearity in both $\theta$ and $z$ conflicts with the intrinsic geometry of the sphere, resulting in distorted inter-agent spacing.

\section{Other path planning controllers} \label{annex:Other path planning controllers}

This section is dedicated to other controllers that were implemented but deemed worse than the chosen one.

\subsection{Solvers (fmincon)}

The use of numerical solvers was tried as an alternative way of placing the \gls{n_D} drones on the target sphere so that a minimum inter-agent distance is respected, instead of driving them individually to the Fibonacci lattice points \gls{q_desired}. The problem was formulated as a single, static nonlinear program, solved once with \texttt{fmincon}~\cite{MATLAB_fmincon} (\gls{SQP} algorithm), rather than as a receding-horizon controller re-solved at every timestep. The formulation is as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decision variables:} the Cartesian coordinates of the \gls{n_D} points \gls{chaser_pos} to be placed on the sphere, stacked into a single vector
    \begin{equation}
      \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 
        \gls{chaser_pos}_1 & \gls{chaser_pos}_2 & \cdots & \gls{chaser_pos}_{\gls{n_D}} 
      \end{bmatrix}^\top 
      \in 
      \mathbb{R}^{3 \gls{n_D}}, 
      \qquad 
      \gls{chaser_i_pos} \in \mathbb{R}^3.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Cost function:} the objective is identically zero, $J(\mathbf{x}) = 0$. No distance-to-target or effort term is minimized; the solver is used purely to find \emph{any} point that satisfies the constraints below, starting from the drones' current positions \gls{chaser_i_pos} as the initial guess.
    \item \textbf{Constraints:}
    \begin{align}
        & \|\mathbf{p}_i\| - \gls{r_desired} = 0, \qquad i = 1,\dots,\gls{n_D}\\
        \intertext{(each point lies on the sphere of radius $\gls{r_desired}$)} \notag\\
        & \gls{min_dist} - d_{i,k} \leq 0, \quad i = 1,\dots,\gls{n_D}, \quad k = 1,\dots,K\\
        \intertext{(minimum geodesic distance to the $K=3$ nearest neighbours)}\notag
    \end{align}
    where $d_{i,k}$ is the geodesic distance from point $i$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbor on the sphere, and \gls{min_dist} is the average nearest-neighbor geodesic distance of the target Fibonacci lattice.
    \item \textbf{Horizon and solve frequency:} there is no time horizon in this formulation. The optimization does not model drone dynamics or motion over time, it is a single static feasibility problem, solved once (offline) for a given number of drones \gls{n_D} and radius \gls{r_desired}, not re-solved online at each control step.
\end{itemize}

This approach suffers from a fundamental limitation. Because the nonlinear constraints above are only guaranteed to hold at the converged solution \gls{p_desired}, the intermediate iterates produced by the \gls{SQP} algorithm along the way are, in general, infeasible: they neither lie exactly on the sphere nor respect the minimum-distance constraint. Figure~\ref{fig.4:solver_solution} plots exactly these intermediate \gls{SQP} iterates, captured through \texttt{fmincon}'s \texttt{OutputFcn} at each optimizer iteration, connected in sequence. The solver does not take the physical trajectory of the drones moving through time and space only a visualization of the solver's internal search history. Consequently, the paths shown do not represent motion that respects collision avoidance, and no claim of continuous constraint satisfaction during a trajectory can be made from this figure, only the final configuration (marked with a cross) is guaranteed to satisfy both constraints. Since the problem is solved once, as a static placement, and not repeatedly along a receding horizon, this method cannot be used directly as an online, constraint-respecting motion planner, it can only guarantee a feasible final configuration.

\subsection{Artificial Potential Field (APF)}

The concept of \gls{APF} was previously introduced in
Section~\ref{sec.3:Artificial Potential Fields}. In this section, it is
applied to enforce inter-agent separation while maintaining a desired
spatial distribution on a spherical surface. The idea is not to drive \gls{q} to its references, but to compute the average distance between the Fibonacci points \gls{p_desired} and let \gls{q} spread until that spacing is reached. Let \gls{n_D} denote the number of drones, and consider the set $\{ \gls{p_i_desired} \in \Gamma^2 \}_{i=1}^{\gls{n_D}}$ representing Fibonacci lattice points distributed on a sphere of radius \gls{r_desired}. From this points the pairwise geodesic distance matrix $\gls{d_ij_geo}$ is computed according to
\begin{equation}
  \gls{d_ij_geo} =
  \begin{cases}
    \gls{r_desired} \arccos \! 
    \left( 
      \dfrac{\gls{p_i_desired}^{\top}\mathbf{p}^\star_j}{\gls{r_desired}^2} 
    \right), 
    & i \neq j, \\[6pt]
    0, & i = j ,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq.4:geodesic_distance}
\end{equation}
from which the average inter-agent distance, defined as the minimum distance for repulsion to occur, is defined as
\begin{equation}
  \gls{d_min} = \frac{1}{\gls{n_D}} \sum_{i=1}^{\gls{n_D}} 
  \left( 
    \frac{1}{\gls{n_D}-1} \sum_{\substack{j=1 \\ j \neq i}}^{\gls{n_D}} \gls{d_ij_geo} 
  \right).
  \label{eq.4:d_avg between drones}
\end{equation}
This quantity is used as a minimum safety distance. 

At each simulation step \gls{time_step}, the Cartesian position $\gls{chaser_i_pos} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ of each drone is projected onto the spherical manifold as
\begin{equation}
  \gls{q_i} = \Pi^{1,\gls{target_pos}}(\gls{chaser_i_pos})
  \label{eq.4:project_to_sphere}
\end{equation}
ensuring that all agents evolve on the surface of the sphere. For each drone $i$, a neighborhood set is defined as
\begin{equation}
  \gls{node_neighbour_set}(k) = \left\{ j \neq i \mid \gls{d_ij_geo}(k) < \gls{d_min} \right\},
\end{equation}
which includes only those agents that violate the minimum separation
constraint. 

The repulsive artificial potential applied to drone $i$ is given by
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{u}_i(k) =
  \sum_{j \in \gls{node_neighbour_set}}
  \left(
  \frac{\gls{q_i}(k) - \mathbf{q}_j(k)}{\|\gls{q_i}(k) - \mathbf{q}_j(k)\|}
  \left( \gls{d_min} - \gls{d_ij_geo}(k) \right)
  \right).
\end{equation}
The term
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\gls{q_i}(k) - \mathbf{q}_j(k)}{\|\gls{q_i}(k) - \mathbf{q}_j(k)\|}
\end{equation*}
defines the unit vector pointing from drone $j$ to drone $i$, while the magnitude $(\gls{d_min} - \gls{d_ij_geo}(k))$ increases linearly as the distance between the drones decreases below the safety threshold. This results in a symmetric repulsive interaction that pushes neighboring agents apart. To avoid excessively large corrections, the resulting vector is saturated as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{u}_i(k) \leftarrow
  \min\!\left( \|\mathbf{u}_i(k)\|,\, \gls{d_min} \right)
  \frac{\mathbf{u}_i(k)}{\|\mathbf{u}_i(k)\|}
  \label{eq.4:saturation_apf}
\end{equation}
which ensures that the displacement applied at each step does not exceed \gls{d_min}. The corrected reference position is then projected back onto the sphere, guaranteeing consistency with the manifold constraint.

\paragraph{fmincon solver.} Unlike the other two approaches, the \texttt{fmincon}-based solver does not pursue a single terminal convergence condition; rather, it seeks to maintain a prescribed inter-agent spacing while driving each agent toward the desired sphere of radius \gls{r_desired}. As can be seen by the tracking-goal error the the time to goal of less then \SI{0.1}{\meter} does not happen in 30 seconds. In addition, the Mellinger controller was unable to accurately track the reference trajectory generated by this solver, exhibiting a reference-tracking error as high as \SI{1.1673}{\meter}. It should be noted that the reported tracking-goal error is not equivalent to the reference-tracking error, although the two are closely related, since the former measures distance to the final objective while the latter measures deviation from the commanded trajectory.

\paragraph{Artificial Potential Field (APF).} The \gls{APF} controller reached the target sphere radius in \SI{4.460}{\second}, with agents maintaining a geodesic spacing of \SI{3.3397}{\meter} from one another. Its total computation time of \SI{9.249}{\second} was nearly three times shorter than the simulated time horizon. The Mellinger controller tracked the \gls{APF}-generated reference with extremely high precision, achieving reference-tracking errors on the order of $10^{-11}$~\si{\meter}. Note, however, that the reported average and maximum tracking errors may be misleading: since the system spends roughly 5/6\textsuperscript{th} of the simulation time near the final desired location, an early period of high tracking error can be masked by a mean that is dominated by near-zero error.

\paragraph{Lyapunov-based controller.} The Lyapunov-based controller likewise achieved a Mellinger reference-tracking error of effectively zero (on the order of $10^{-4}$~\si{\meter}), although it required a longer convergence time of \SI{17.610}{\second}. This behavior is consistent with the underlying control law, which first aligns the agents' angular position \gls{q} on the two-sphere with the desired orientation before reducing the radial distance to the target value, in contrast to the \gls{APF}, which does not enforce this two-stage behavior. A further advantage of this controller is that it allows the desired position to be directly specified on the two-sphere. Despite its longer convergence time, it required less computation time than the \gls{APF} approach, at \SI{7.826}{\second}.

\paragraph{Discussion.} In light of the above results, the Lyapunov-based controller was selected as the most well-rounded choice for the objectives of this thesis. It achieves near-zero reference-tracking error while requiring less computation time than the \gls{APF} controller. Although its convergence time is comparatively higher, this is a direct consequence of the controller's strategy of first aligning the axis \gls{q} with the desired orientation before reducing the radial distance. This behavior is also considered elegant from a geometric standpoint, as the target is first encircled and subsequently enclosed within a sphere of the desired radius \gls{r_desired}.

\section{Other iterative computation agent spacing on the two-sphere} \label{annex:Other iterative computation agent spacing on the two-sphere}

As has been previously stated the distinction between direct and iterative methods matters because iterative methods allow or the use of decentralized implementation of agent spacing that might even not require for every agent to know how many agents exist in the formation as well as not have to agree on the position each agent will take.

This section introduces other methods that were implemented but considered inferior to the one used in \ref{sec.5:Iterative computation agent spacing on the two-sphere} followed by the comparison and understanding of the choice of distribution.

\subsection{Thomson minimum energy sphere} \label{annex:Thomson minimum energy sphere}

In 1904, J. J. Thomson proposed a model for the atom in which electrons were thought to be evenly distributed within a positively charged sphere. In this model, each electron experiences repulsive forces from the others and is constrained to remain on the surface of the sphere. This concept led to what is now known as the Thomson problem, which seeks the arrangement of \gls{n_D} electrons on the surface of a unit sphere that minimizes their total electrostatic potential energy, given that they repel each other according to Coulomb's law. Thomson introduced this problem while developing his atomic model, later called the plum pudding model, reflecting the understanding that negatively charged electrons exist within a neutrally charged atom.\cite{thomson_min_energy_sphere}

The Thomson problem can be formulated mathematically as the minimization of the total Coulomb energy of \gls{n_D} point charges constrained to the surface of the unit sphere. Let $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the Cartesian coordinates of the $i$-th point on the sphere, such that $\|\mathbf{x}_i\| = 1$ for all $i = 1, \dots, \gls{n_D}$. The total electrostatic potential energy is given by

\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^{\gls{n_D}} \sum_{j=i+1}^{\gls{n_D}} \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{\gls{n_D}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times \gls{n_D}}$ is the matrix of all point coordinates, and $\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2$ is the Euclidean distance between points $i$ and $j$. The goal of the Thomson problem is to find

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}^* = \text{arg} \min_{\mathbf{X}} E(\mathbf{X}).
\end{equation}
The only found benefit of using this method, for the desired formation purposes, is that due to there not being any imposed  restrictions, the number of agents on the two-sphere can be any \gls{n_D}. The results of this method can be visible in Figure~\ref{fig.3:thomson_demonstration}.

\paragraph{Thomson (minimal energy)} This method never converged within the tolerance across all 100 runs; because no run converged, no mean iterations-to-convergence value is defined (Table~\ref{tab.5:method_comparison_convergence}), indicating the method approaches but does not settle into a stable equilibrium under this convergence criterion within the 1000-iteration limit. Its spacing quality is also markedly worse than the other two methods: $d_{NN,\min}/d_{NN,avg} = 0.3246$ is far from the ideal of 1.0, $CV(d_{NN}) = 0.6950$, and its Riesz energy (461.79) is the highest of the three, confirming a comparatively uneven and energetically unfavorable distribution.

\paragraph{Lloyd (convex hull)} This method converges universally (100/100 runs) in a mean of 20.470 iterations, making it both the fastest-converging and the only method with a perfect convergence rate in this test. Its spacing metrics are close to those of the half-plane variant ($d_{NN,\min}/d_{NN,avg} = 0.8585$ vs.\ $0.8486$; Riesz energy 363.06 vs.\ 360.77), with only a modestly higher $CV(d_{NN})$ (0.1202 vs.\ 0.0728).

\paragraph{Lloyd (half plane)} This method produces the most uniform spacing of the three, the lowest $CV(d_{NN})$ (0.0728) and lowest Riesz energy (360.77), but at a steep computational cost: a mean run time of 1.367 s (about $41\times$ slower than the convex-hull method and $16\times$ slower than the Thomson method), and a lower convergence rate (93/100) requiring more iterations on average (29.110) when it does converge.

\paragraph{Final analysis} In short, Thomson's method is unsuitable under the present convergence criterion despite its low per-run cost, since it never converges and yields the poorest spacing. The two Lloyd variants are much closer contenders: convex hull offers substantially faster run time than half plane with a perfect, reliable convergence, while half plane offers a further, but comparatively small, improvement in spacing uniformity at a disproportionate cost in run time and a slightly reduced convergence rate. Given that the spacing-quality gap between the two Lloyd variants is small relative to the order-of-magnitude difference in run time, both are retained for full system-level testing of the control architecture, so that this trade-off can be assessed under simulation operating conditions rather than resolved on run time alone.

\section{Comparison between the spherical CVT methods and the Dijkstra-based CVT} \label{annex:Comparison between the spherical CVT methods and the Dijkstra-based CVT}

Chapter~\ref{cha:Decentralized Development} computes the \gls{CVT} with two constructions that only exist on the two-sphere, the convex hull and the half plane (Section~\ref{sec.5:Iterative computation agent spacing on the two-sphere}), whereas Chapter~\ref{cha:Generalized Development} replaces them with a Lloyd iteration whose Voronoi regions are obtained with Dijkstra's algorithm on a triangle mesh (Sections~\ref{sec.6:Iterative computation agent spacing on the two-sphere} and~\ref{sec.6:goal_assignment}). The two-sphere is the only surface on which the three methods can be applied, so this section compares them there, in order to quantify what is gained and what is lost when moving from the spherical constructions to the mesh-based one.

\subsection{Differences between the methods}

Table~\ref{tab.annex:cvt_properties} summarizes how the three methods differ. The convex-hull and half-plane methods place the sites anywhere on the sphere, while the Dijkstra method restricts the sites to the vertices of the mesh and measures distances along its edges. The Dijkstra method is therefore an approximation in two ways: the positions of the sites are quantized to the mesh resolution, and the graph distance overestimates the geodesic distance, since a path can only follow mesh edges. In exchange, it is the only one of the three that does not depend on the geometry of the sphere, and it accepts the vertex weights used in Section~\ref{sec:velocity_weighting}.

\subsection{Test conditions}

The conditions are those of Section~\ref{annex:Comparison of Agent Distributions on the Two-Sphere}: 30 sites on the unit sphere, 100 runs, at most 1000 iterations per run, and a run converges when no site moves more than $5\times10^{-3}$~m (tol) between two iterations. Each run starts from random points on the sphere, and the same initial points are given to the three methods; for the Dijkstra method each initial point is moved to the closest mesh vertex. All sites are updated in every iteration, that is, the methods are run with full knowledge of the other sites, as in Section~\ref{annex:Comparison of Agent Distributions on the Two-Sphere}.

The mesh of the Dijkstra method is the one of Section~\ref{sec.6:Iterative computation agent spacing on the two-sphere}: the vertices are a Fibonacci lattice on the unit sphere and the faces are its convex hull. The main comparison uses 3001 vertices (mean edge length 0.071), and the effect of the mesh resolution is evaluated separately with 1001 and 10001 vertices. The implementation is the one used in the MATLAB simulations of this thesis, a binary-heap Dijkstra over an array of vertex objects, whose adjacency lists are rebuilt in every iteration because in Chapter~\ref{cha:Generalized Development} the mesh changes between iterations. Since tol is smaller than the edge length of every mesh, for the Dijkstra method the convergence criterion is equivalent to no site changing vertex.

The three methods were run again for this section, on the computer of Section~\ref{appendix:Computer Specifications} under Linux with MATLAB R2024a, so the values of the convex-hull and half-plane methods differ slightly from those of Tables~\ref{tab.5:method_comparison_convergence} and~\ref{tab.5:method_comparison_spacing}, which come from a different set of runs. The spacing metrics are here averaged over the runs instead of being taken from one representative run.

\paragraph{Run time} The Dijkstra method is the slowest of the three. One iteration takes 0.66~s, about 600 times longer than one iteration of the convex hull and about 37 times longer than one of the half plane, and a run takes 6.3~s on average. Part of this cost belongs to the implementation and not to the algorithm, since the vertex objects and their adjacency lists are rebuilt in every iteration, but the cost of the search itself also grows with the number of mesh vertices, while the cost of the other two methods depends only on the number of sites. This is the reason why in Chapter~\ref{cha:Generalized Development} the \gls{CVT} is the most expensive module and runs at a lower rate than the rest of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Convergence} The Dijkstra method converged in all 100 runs and needed the fewest iterations (9.49 on average, against 20.26 and 29.66). This does not mean that it is closer to the continuous \gls{CVT}: the sites can only move from vertex to vertex, so a centroid displacement smaller than the vertex spacing does not move the site and the iteration stops earlier. Because the positions are discrete, the iteration could also return to a configuration already visited and repeat it indefinitely; this was monitored and did not occur in any run. The half plane failed to converge in 6 runs. In all 6 the positions became undefined: a cell contained no element of the sampled sphere, so its centroid was divided by a zero area. These runs then used the 1000 iterations, which explains the difference between the mean (1.545~s) and the median (0.511~s) run time of this method.

\paragraph{Spacing quality} With 3001 vertices the Dijkstra method gives a distribution as uniform as that of the convex hull ($CV(d_{NN})$ of 0.109 against 0.118, with similar ratio and Riesz energy) and less uniform than that of the half plane (0.061). The discretization is therefore not what limits the quality at this resolution and number of sites.

\subsection{Final analysis}

On the two-sphere the Dijkstra method has no advantage over the spherical methods of Chapter~\ref{cha:Decentralized Development}: at equal uniformity it is two orders of magnitude slower than the convex hull, and reaching the uniformity of the half plane requires a mesh fine enough to make it about 80 times slower than the half plane in median run time. For a formation that is always a sphere, the methods of Chapter~\ref{cha:Decentralized Development} remain the better choice. The Dijkstra method is used in Chapter~\ref{cha:Generalized Development} because it is the only one of the three that works on a surface that is not a sphere, such as the disc, the cone and the intermediate shapes produced by the mesh controller, and because it accepts the vertex weights of the velocity-dependent metric. It also showed the most predictable behaviour of the three in this test, converging in every run and never producing the degenerate configurations of the convex hull or the undefined centroids of the half plane. The results of this section show that its distribution on the sphere is comparable to that of the convex-hull method when the mesh has about 100 vertices per site, so the generalization to arbitrary meshes costs computation time and not formation quality.
